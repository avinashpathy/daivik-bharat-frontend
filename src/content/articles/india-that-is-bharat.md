## The question

Most of us use "India" and "Bharat" as if they were the same word. In 2023 a single dinner invitation turned that into a national argument. So where do the two names actually come from, and what is the argument about?

## What the sources say

**The Constitution names the country twice.** Its first article reads:

> India, that is Bharat, shall be a Union of States.
> — Article 1(1), Constitution of India

Both names are official, and neither is defined as a translation of the other.

**"Bharat" comes from older Indian texts.** The Vishnu Purana (2.3.1) describes Bhāratavarṣa as the land north of the ocean and south of the snowy mountains, where the descendants of Bharata live:

> उत्तरं यत्समुद्रस्य हिमाद्रेश्चैव दक्षिणम् ।
> वर्षं तद्भारतं नाम भारती यत्र सन्ततिः ॥

The texts don't agree on which Bharata. The Mahabharata traces the line to Bharata, son of Dushyanta and Shakuntala. The Bhagavata Purana and Jain tradition name Bharata, the eldest son of Rishabhadeva. Even earlier, the Rigveda mentions the Bharatas as a tribe; their king Sudas wins the Battle of the Ten Kings in Mandala 7, hymn 18.

**"India" comes from the river Sindhu, or Indus.** Persian speakers said "Hindu", the Greeks wrote "Indos", and the word reached Latin and English as "India". It was in use for well over two thousand years before British rule.

**The wording was debated in 1949.** When the Constituent Assembly took up Article 1 on 18 September 1949, H. V. Kamath proposed "Bharat, or in the English language, India", and members including Seth Govind Das argued for putting Bharat first. The amendment was defeated, and the current wording was adopted.

## How people see it today

In September 2023, invitations to a G20 dinner described the host as the "President of Bharat" instead of the usual "President of India". The reactions split roughly along these lines:

- **Those who welcomed it** said "Bharat" is an indigenous name with deep roots, that it is already in the Constitution, and that using it is a step away from colonial-era habits.
- **Those who objected** said the change was made without debate, that Article 52 says "There shall be a President of India", and some suspected it was aimed at the opposition alliance that had named itself INDIA a few weeks earlier.
- **Many people in between** pointed out that both names are constitutional and in everyday use, and asked why it had to be either-or.

## What's still unclear

- No proposal to amend Article 1 has been formally made, as far as we could find. If that changes, we'll update this page.
- How much the two names carry different meanings for different groups of Indians is a question of opinion, not record. We've tried to report the arguments, not settle them.
